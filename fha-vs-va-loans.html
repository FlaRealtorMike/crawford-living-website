FHA vs VA Loans: Which One Fits You?

Both can get you into a home with less money down, but they work very differently. Here they are side by side.

Video

Prefer YouTube? Watch it there.

The short version

  • FHA: as little as 3.5% down with a qualifying credit score, and more forgiving credit requirements.
  • FHA has an upfront and an annual mortgage insurance premium. With less than 10% down, the monthly premium stays for the life of the loan.
  • VA: an earned benefit for many veterans, active-duty members and some surviving spouses. Often no down payment and no monthly mortgage insurance.
  • VA has a one-time funding fee that can usually be rolled into the loan; some buyers, including many veterans with a service-connected disability, are exempt.
  • With solid credit, ask your lender to price a conventional loan too. Both FHA and VA have property standards.

What to do next

  1. If you've served, request your Certificate of Eligibility early.
  2. Ask your lender to price FHA, VA (if eligible) and conventional on the same day.
  3. Compare the monthly payment and the total cost, not just the down payment.

Questions

How much do you need to put down on an FHA loan?

As little as 3.5% with a qualifying credit score.

Does FHA mortgage insurance ever go away?

If you put down less than 10%, the monthly mortgage insurance stays for the life of the loan; the way out is to sell or refinance.

Does a VA loan have mortgage insurance?

No monthly mortgage insurance. There is a one-time funding fee, which can usually be rolled into the loan, and some veterans are exempt.

If you're buying your first home, or you've served in the military, you'll probably hear about two loans. FHA and VA. Both can get you into a home with less money down. But they work very differently. And picking the wrong one can cost you thousands.

Let's lay them side by side. An FHA loan is insured by the federal government, which lets lenders be more flexible. You can put down as little as 3.5% with a qualifying credit score, and the credit requirements are more forgiving than most conventional loans. That's why FHA is popular with first-time buyers who are still building savings or credit. The trade-off is mortgage insurance.

With FHA, you pay an upfront premium, which is usually rolled into the loan, plus an annual premium that's split into your monthly payment. And here's the part people miss. If you put down less than 10%, that monthly mortgage insurance stays for the life of the loan. The only way out is to sell or refinance. So FHA can be a great door in.

But it's worth comparing to a conventional loan, where mortgage insurance eventually comes off. A VA loan is a benefit earned through military service. It's available to many veterans, active duty service members, and some surviving spouses. For qualified buyers, it often requires no down payment at all, and there's no monthly mortgage insurance. That combination makes it one of the strongest loans available anywhere.

VA loans do have a one-time funding fee. It can usually be rolled into the loan, and the amount depends on your down payment and whether you've used the benefit before. Some buyers don't pay it at all, including many veterans who receive compensation for a service-connected disability. Your lender can tell you exactly where you land. So, how do you choose?

If you're eligible for VA, start there. It's very hard to beat no down payment and no monthly mortgage insurance. If you're not eligible, FHA can be a strong fit when your credit is still growing or your savings are thin. And if your credit is solid, ask your lender to price a conventional loan too. With a good score, it may cost less, over time.

Here's what I do. If you've served, request your certificate of eligibility early. Ask your lender to show you, FHA, VA if you qualify, and conventional, all on the same day, so you can compare the monthly payment and the total cost. And, remember that both FHA and VA have property standards, so the home itself needs to be in decent shape.
