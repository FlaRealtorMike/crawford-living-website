Florida Property Taxes: Homestead, Save Our Homes and Portability

Property taxes are one of the biggest surprises in a Florida payment, and Florida also has some of the best breaks for people who live in their home full-time.

The short version

  • Your bill: assessed value, minus exemptions, times local millage rates, plus flat charges such as trash, stormwater and sometimes a CDD fee.
  • The tax bill on a listing belongs to the current owner. After you buy, the home is reassessed at market value as of the next January 1.
  • Homestead exemption: own and live in the home as your primary residence on January 1, and apply with your county property appraiser by March 1. Filing is free.
  • Save Our Homes caps yearly increases in assessed value on a homestead at 3% or inflation, whichever is lower.
  • Portability can carry much of those built-up savings to your next Florida homestead. It isn't automatic; request it when you file.

What to do next

  1. Estimate taxes on your purchase price, not the seller's bill.
  2. File for homestead by March 1 after you move in, and ask about portability if you sold a Florida homestead.
  3. Pay early in November for the biggest discount if you pay the bill yourself.

Questions

When is the Florida homestead exemption deadline?

You must own and live in the home as your permanent residence on January 1, and apply with your county property appraiser by March 1.

What is the Save Our Homes cap?

Once your homestead is in place, the assessed value can rise no more than 3% a year or the rate of inflation, whichever is lower.

What is homestead portability?

If you sell a Florida homestead and buy another in Florida, you may be able to transfer much of your built-up Save Our Homes savings. You request it when you file homestead on the new home.

If you're buying a home in Florida, property taxes can be one of the biggest surprises in your payment. The good news is that Florida also has some of the best tax breaks in the country for people who live in their home full-time. Let's walk through how your bill works, and how to make sure you're not paying more than you should. A Florida tax bill comes together in a few steps. The county property appraiser sets your assessed value.

Exemptions come off that value. What's left is multiplied by the local tax rates, called millage, from the county, the city, the schools, and other districts. Then flat charges get added, like trash pickup, stormwater, and sometimes a CDD fee. Those flat charges don't change with your home's value, so read them separately. Here's the surprise for a lot of buyers.

The tax bill you see on a listing belongs to the current owner. If they've lived there for years with their exemptions and caps, their bill may be far lower than yours will be. After you buy, the home is reassessed at its market value as of the next January 1st. So when you plan your budget, estimate taxes on your purchase price, not on the seller's bill. If the home is your permanent, primary residence, you can apply for the Florida Homestead Exemption.

It takes a meaningful amount off the taxable value of your home. You need to own and live in the home as of January 1, and you apply with your county property appraiser by March 1. It's free to apply, and in Orange County you can do it online. Don't let a mailer convince you to pay someone to file it for you. Once your homestead is in place, you also get the Save Our Homes cap.

It limits how much your assessed value can go up each year to 3% or the rate of inflation, whichever is lower, even if the market jumps much more. Over time, that gap between your capped value and the market value can add up to real savings. And if you sell your Florida Homestead and buy another one in Florida, you may be able to bring a big part of those built-up savings with you. That's called portability. It isn't automatic.

You request it when you file Homestead on your new home, so be sure to ask the property appraiser about it. Three quick tips. Florida tax bills go out in the fall, and paying early earns a small discount, with the biggest discount in November. If you have a mortgage, your lender usually pays the bill from your escrow account, and laws and amounts can change. So always check the current figures with your county property appraiser.
