Buying a home

How Mortgage Rates Actually Work

Nobody knows exactly where rates are headed. But a lot about your own rate is in your hands. Here is how rates are set and the levers you control.

Video

Prefer YouTube? Watch it there.

The short version

  • The Federal Reserve doesn't set mortgage rates. They tend to follow the bond market and inflation expectations.
  • Five things move your rate: credit score, down payment, loan type, loan length, and shopping more than one lender.
  • Compare both the interest rate and the APR. A very low rate with a much higher APR usually means you're paying fees up front.
  • Points lower your rate for the life of the loan; they pay off only if you keep the loan long enough. Temporary buy-downs step up after a year or two.
  • Buy a home whose payment works today. A future refinance is a bonus, not a plan.

What to do next

  1. Check your credit before you shop.
  2. Get official Loan Estimates from at least two or three lenders on the same day.
  3. Compare the rate and the APR, and ask what it costs to lower the rate.

Questions

Does the Federal Reserve set mortgage rates?

No. The Fed sets a short-term rate banks charge each other. Mortgage rates tend to follow long-term government bonds and inflation expectations.

What is the difference between the interest rate and the APR?

The interest rate sets your monthly payment. The APR folds in many of the loan's fees, so it shows a fuller picture of the cost.

Are mortgage points worth it?

A point is a fee paid at closing to lower your rate for the life of the loan. It tends to pay off only if you keep the loan for years; if you may move or refinance soon, it may not.

Everybody wants to know where mortgage rates are headed. Here's the honest answer. Nobody knows for sure. Not your lender. Not the news.

And not me. But there's a lot about your rate that you can control. So let's talk about how rates actually work, and the levers that are in your hands. First, a common myth. The Federal Reserve doesn't set mortgage rates.

The Fed sets a short-term rate that banks charge each other. Mortgage rates tend to follow the bond market, especially long-term government bonds, along with what investors expect inflation to do. When inflation worries go up, rates usually go up. When the economy cools, rates often ease. That's the big picture, and none of us can steer it.

So let's spend our time on what you can. Your rate is built on top of that market rate, and five things push yours up or down. 1. Your credit score. Higher scores usually earn better pricing.

2. Your down payment. More down often means a lower rate or less mortgage insurance. 3. The type of loan, like conventional, FHA or VA.

4. The length of the loan. A 15-year loan usually has a lower rate than a 30-year, but a bigger monthly payment. 5. Shopping around.

Rates and fees can differ from lender to lender on the very same day, so get quotes from at least two or three. When you compare offers, look at two numbers. The interest rate decides your monthly payment. The APR, the annual percentage rate, folds in many of the loan's fees, so it shows the fuller cost. A quote with a very low rate and a much higher APR usually means you're paying for that rate with fees up front.

Ask for the official loan estimate from each lender. It's a standard form, so it's easy to compare side by side. You'll hear about points. A point is a fee you pay at closing to lower your rate for the life of the loan. Whether it's worth it depends on how long you'll keep the loan.

If you'll likely move or refinance in a few years, points may not pay off. There's also a temporary buy-down, where the rate starts lower for the first year or two, then steps up. Sometimes a seller will pay for one as part of the deal. Just make sure you can afford the full payment once it steps up. Here's the part I'd tell a friend.

Don't buy a home counting on a future refinance to make the payment work. Rates might drop, and refinancing could help. But it's not guaranteed, and it has its own costs. Buy a home whose payment fits your budget today. If rates fall later, that's a bonus, not the plan.

So here's what I'd do, check your credit before you shop, get loan estimates from at least two or three lenders on the same day, compare the rate and the APR, and ask each lender what it would cost to lower your rate so you can decide with real numbers.
