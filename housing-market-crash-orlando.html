Questions

Is the housing market going to crash like 2008?

No one can predict prices, but the conditions behind 2008 (loose lending and resetting adjustable rates) are different today: repayment is verified, most loans are fixed-rate, and many owners have equity.

What's the difference between a crash and a correction?

A correction is prices flattening or slipping in some areas while others hold steady. A crash is a fast, broad drop driven by forced sales.

What local numbers should I watch?

How many homes are for sale, how long they take to sell, and how often sellers are cutting prices in your neighborhood.

It might be the question I hear most — is the housing market going to crash? Here's my honest answer. Nobody can tell you exactly what prices will do next year, but we can look at what actually causes a crash, what's different today, and how to make a smart decision no matter which way the market moves. To talk about a crash, it helps to remember the last big one. Before 2008, many loans were made without checking whether people could really afford them.

Lots of buyers had adjustable rates that jumped after a few years. When payments rose, many owners couldn't keep up, and a wave of forced sales and foreclosures pushed prices down fast. Today's picture is different in a few important ways. After 2008, lending rules tightened, and lenders now have to verify that you can repay the loan. Most homeowners have fixed-rate mortgages, so their payment doesn't suddenly jump.

And many owners have built up real equity. None of that means prices can't fall. It just means the conditions that caused 2008 aren't the same. There's a big difference between a crash and a correction. Prices can flatten or slip in some neighborhoods, while others hold steady.

Real estate is local. Here in central Florida, a single-family home in an established neighborhood can behave very differently from a condo, where new safety rules, reserves and insurance costs play a bigger role. If you're buying, the question to ask isn't whether the market will dip. It's whether this home fits your life and your budget for the next several years. If you plan to stay at least five years and the payment is comfortable, short-term ups and downs matter a lot less.

The real risk is stretching too far. So keep a cushion. If you're selling, focus on today's market, not last year's peak or next year's guess. Price your home for the buyers who are shopping right now. And if you're buying your next home in the same area, remember that both sides tend to move together.

A softer price on your sale often means a softer price on your purchase too. Headlines are usually about the national market. What matters to you is your neighborhood. Watch a few local numbers. How many homes are for sale, how long they're taking to sell, and how often sellers are cutting prices.
